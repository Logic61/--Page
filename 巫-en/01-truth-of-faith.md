The faith spoken of here is faith in ghosts and gods.

Gods come in two kinds. One is conjured from nothing: the collected minds of the multitude form a vast spiritual force, and these incense-fire thought-powers blend together; according to what people hold in common about a certain god, a god is made out of thin air. The more people believe this god has a certain trait, the more that trait becomes his chief office. Divine power and divine office — all of it is granted by believers. If the people's incense-fire thought-power makes a plague god, it grants him the power to spread plagues; if it makes a sea god, it grants him the power to stir up waves. The more people believe, the more incense-fire thought-power there is, and the stronger this god becomes. But such a god has no self at all — like a computer, it only gives back information when you type something in with the keyboard. A single believer's influence on it is tiny, but the faith of countless believers all but rules everything about this god. A god condensed of incense-fire is a thing with intelligence but no self-awareness — perhaps over measureless ages it may beget a little dawning, molded self-awareness from its dreams of sorts, but not much. The other kind: the original soul or yin-spirit still exists, and such a god has a self. Perhaps for some reason people took him for a god; perhaps an emperor enfeoffed him as one — at any rate he possesses incense-fire, and with the spiritual support of believers he becomes a god.

This kind of god is also greatly affected by incense-fire, but keeps some self-awareness — also not much, and it wears away bit by bit. At first this self-awareness is close to the original person, but gradually he is no longer entirely that person. Contaminated by vast incense-fire, it is like pouring a bottle of cola into a glass of milk — is it still the same glass of milk? Yet you cannot say it is wholly not; the milk has merely gone bad. So most gods are a lump of cloud servers; long life or not means little. Only a minority truly possess a true spirit and some self.

Worth noting: ancestor worship, and the quasi-ancestor worship of ancient sages and literary men, are admiration and remembrance of forebears — comparatively pure, far less harmful. Even when a descendant says "ancestors, bless us," most do not truly expect the ancestors to manifest anything. So Confucius eats that bowl of incense-fire quite contentedly.

So long as one believer remains, the god does not die — at the least a seed remains; if more people believe in him, he can come back from the dead. Some died ages ago, their souls long scattered, yet were enfeoffed as gods or revered as gods through some circumstance: that god is then no longer that person. He is only the person in everyone's hearts — but the god himself wholly believes he is that person.

"Man lives by a breath; the Buddha contends for a stick of incense" — precisely the difference between the human way of cultivation and the divine way. For a man to live long he must first refine qi; for a god to be strong he must have incense-fire. So whether orthodox or heterodox, whether for the god's benefit or the order's, all religions must contend for believers. Sometimes even living shrines arise: to be called a god and receive incense-fire while alive does the body and lifespan no good — may even shorten life — but after death the soul may be kept from scattering. A kind of alternative long life. A god is a special yin-spirit: soul and po scatter, the yin-spirit has its span, but a god with faith is eternal — and mightier by far than an ordinary yin-spirit. An ancient earth-god or city-god, if the emperor strips his divine title and smashes his temple and no one believes in him anymore, becomes an ordinary yin-spirit. As a god, whether you believe, praise, hate, or curse him — so long as he is in your heart, so long as you hold him to exist and think of him at some time, he can draw thought-power from you, more or less. What he fears is that you do not care about him at all, that you treat him as nothing. For gods, Buddhas, and ghosts are not material entities — they belong to □□□□□□□□□□□□□ — and so are easily affected by human minds. Conversely, their interference in the physical world is very limited; to affect non-believers is not so easy, unless humans kneel of their own accord. And they must work through the incense-fire link to influence a person's thoughts, and only then indirectly influence things. Hence gods and Buddhas all depend on spreading their teachings to □□□□□□□□; chanting sutras, chanting names, burning incense, bowing to gods are all outer shows.

In truth, the incense-fire that gods and Buddhas most desire: first, fanatics and saints, whose faith-force is purest, ceaseless, low in impurities and side effects. Second, those who cultivate while believing in gods — the thought-power they give is a hundredfold a thousandfold an ordinary believer's, and after death they may become a powerful split-spirit of the god, or even gain for nothing a split-spirit at their own realm. Third, proselytizers: they bring gods and Buddhas more incense-fire faith-power, so preaching is reckoned a great merit in religion.

What gods and Buddhas hate most is the faith-force of those who hate and slander them. As for the cluttered prayers of asking this and begging that — also displeasing to gods and Buddhas — but the general believer is the base, vast in number, and must be accepted. Of course, liking and hating here all presume the god or Buddha has self-awareness.

Incense-fire thought-power is like the currency of the dharma-realm, condensed from everyone's thoughts. The merit of preaching is your deposit, paid out all at once when you die. So though you are not rich in the world, you may be a grandee of the dharma-realm. Only: money earned in the human world is spent in the dharma-realm — don't think you can carry a cent back home. You can pile up merit in the human world, and when you arrive in the dharma-realm be a mogul at once.

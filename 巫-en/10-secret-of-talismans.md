On talismans: some always speak of them mysteriously, so here is basic knowledge of this field, lest you be bluffed stiff, like being blasted by a giant firecracker.

Talismans work much like spells — one may even say a talisman is a spell confined to paper. As spells divide into self-power and divine way, talismans answer in two corresponding kinds: one able to commune with deities and borrow their power, sealing it into the paper; one requiring higher attainment, using one's own essence-qi-spirit, focused and condensed, to lodge one's spirit-qi in the paper.

First, spirit-communication talismans. Materials first — ink alone here: for invoking orthodox gods or high-power deities, yang materials like cinnabar; for non-orthodox gods or ghost-kinds, common ink or yin ink.

With materials ready, step one: purify hands, heart, and mouth, and burn incense, to show respect to the ancestors and deities. More than ritual feel, this step also includes communing with the ancestor — by cunsi, incantation, or silent recital of the ancestor's holy name, contacting the ancestor one would invoke. Step two: after contact, take up the brush and first paint the talisman head. A Daoist communing with the Three Pure Ones' ancestry paints the Three Pure Ones' head: "Grandpa Three Pure Ones, this little Daoist begs a small favor." Below the head is the talisman deity, showing the presiding god invoked. Step three is ordaining the talisman — painting the ordainment character; some skip it, some do not. Ordainment is the decree: issuing orders, mustering or requesting gods or attached troops by the Daoist's own dharma-office authority. If the head reads "I, on the authority of the Supreme Laojun," the ordainment character is "hasten as the law commands". Step four: talisman gall and talisman body, in varying order — the body supports, connects, and beautifies the talisman's outward form, and states in sign or word what the god is asked to do; the gall generally uses taboo-characters to name the divine officers and generals in charge of the matter. Functions of body and gall cross between talismans. Fourth part: talisman foot — generally stating that the presiding deity grants dharma-power and decree, dispatching specific soldiers to do the work; the execution of this talisman falls on those soldiers, enthroned within it. The last step is knotting the sha. There is a saying — draw a talisman without knotting the sha, how did it go, forgotten; anyway it means useless. Forms of knotting vary: painted signs, seals, hand-seals or incantations, other gestures. All serve to grant the talisman dharma-power and strengthen its effect. For instance, some knotting methods cunsi the divine officer of the specific purview, or cunsi the deity transforming into the talisman, then make a certain movement or through certain qi-channels cunsi sending this deity or talisman into the just-drawn talisman, or pouring the deity-power in. For instance, let the deity-power walk from the brain to the hand, into the talisman — thus the talisman is given its motive force.

By analogy: you are an imperial envoy. To get something done, you take the token the emperor gave you (the talisman head) and notify the governor (the presiding god), requiring him to order the commandant (the gall) to send a centurion with soldiers (the foot) to arrest the thief at your command (the body). You stress to centurion and soldiers that the commandant's order is death-stern, execution for disobedience (knotting the sha). Some envoys have attached guards of their own (memorializing office and receiving registers); some may execute first and report later (Celestial Masters) — no need for such mazes. You see: working talismans is not fighting and killing, but human relations. If your god-links are poor, no one does the work for you. Not all the above steps are needed, nor complete, but generally nothing escapes these. Normally they require cultivating cunsi or chanting — to greet the gods in advance for easier contact later. And drawing requires no less seriousness. These are the root of talismans. As for the pictures and characters — agreed conventions, not only within Daoism but with ghosts and gods; and one must practice much, lest a painting error make them not work. There are many kinds of spirit-communication talismans, painted differently by different sects. There is also the incense-script talisman, painted from the traces of burning incense when praying and making vows to the ancestors.

Forms differ, but the reasoning connects.

Such spirit-communication talismans generally need divine office, a seed, passed method, or school membership for good effect; without divine office, sincere worship and many daily kowtows can also get a seed — some slight effect. Of talisman specifics I know little, only principles. Drawing differs much between schools; some may even paint by direct possession. With authority and office one still communes by cunsi and chant, or the effect differs. But with authority and office, one may also muddle through carelessly, sparing effort. Drawing every one seriously drains much spirit-qi; accomplished Daoists dislike drawing many — some draw only a few such taxing talismans a year. So best not to play with this; it all concerns ghosts and gods, and has a price.

Self-power talismans arose earlier, but talisman-masters' attainment was low and effects poor, so they never developed well. Self-power talismans trace at the earliest to knot-records and sign-records; their birth coincides with the birth of writing. Rope knots, signs, characters — the most primitive talismans, essentially information carriers. The base of such talismans' power is the world's common recognition of what the characters mean — the carrier of the multitude's thought-power, the same principle as god-making.

Before and after drawing, the master may use his spirit and essence to strengthen the effect — the source of self-power talismans' reinforcement; the user may do the same. Setting base power aside, one may define signs at will, powering them with one's own spirit-qi alone.

Once a person is famous, the crowd holds various views and thoughts of him, good and bad — akin to incense-fire thought, they form a kind of □□ around the person, joining with the □□ outside his body. This has a good side: raising one's qi-field, lifting one's spirit, boosting one's fortune. But despite the body's isolation it still affects thinking somewhat, and as it can strengthen fortune it can ruin it as well. Pointed at by a thousand fingers, dying without illness — a more extreme saying of it.

Those who run sect genealogies do it to enjoy some incense-fire faith-force after death — failing cultivation, at least to linger alive.

But those who in life found religions, preach, spread the school, or win great fame are either cultivating for godhood, or of great attainment and neither fearing nor touching incense-fire, or they end up the wretched thing said above — rather than a quiet death. From this view, the ancestral divine way is in fact very clever: a few descendants suffice to maintain it; descendants inheriting keep one undissolved in death; many sons and grandsons and one lives comfortably with some self-awareness. Only after several generations may earlier ones — featureless, deedless, name only — lose independence and merge into a common "ancestor" body, no longer existing apart.

The Confucians' merit, words, virtue — an exploration on the ancestral base: borrowing outsiders' thoughts beyond descendants to become a kind of alternate ancestral god — barring too great a fame, which overreaches. The □□ patriarchal genealogy is another attempt: turning the ancestral god into an ancestor-master god; relying not on blood descendants but on disciples' disciples. Blood descendants' thoughts match more closely — but numbers can be controlled; failing attainment, so long as the line does not break and the ancestor's deeds are passed down, one lives quite comfortably. But if some later followers puff the deeds and fame too large, the incense-fire thought turns cluttered; the ancestor gradually becomes a deep-powered deity, not to be □□ — yet leashed by incense-fire all the same.

Do good without nearing fame; do evil without nearing punishment. In life, keep out of prison; in death, keep out of the divine way: Daoist wisdom. The living are affected; most typical is entertainment. Stars at their height glow — they turn prettier, as if ringed with light. Out of fashion, the ring gone, they turn plain, dim, tired. □□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□. These are the influences of spirit and qi.

Daoist self-power cultivators rarely enjoy great fame in their time; recluses avoid fame, untouched by incense-fire. A few children, or a few disciples, to burn paper for you later — no great matter; that is comparatively pure incense, within a few generations at least of light effect on the mind, no mutual enslavement of god and man.

To be unburdened by fame and faith-force takes some attainment. Ghost-immortals and yang-spirits have some resistance; earthly immortals are roughly immune. Before resistance or immunity, once touched, great trouble: the soul grows impure, and the road behind in cultivation is cut.

Opposed to the divine way stands self-power; divine way and self-power are two different roads of cultivation.

Self-power is cultivation that relies on no external means at all, purely tempering one's own spirit-qi to reach such goals as long life and freedom. Some self-power methods temper both form and spirit, some temper the spirit alone, but in every case the spirit-qi finally belongs to oneself. In the language of novels: great power belongs to the self.

The divine way is the reverse. Divine-way methods are those derived from incense-fire thought-power: gathering thought, worshipping gods, seeding, conveying power, empowerment, possession, and the like. Incense-fire here is not incense and flame; incense-fire is the fire of human belief — in the phrasing of D&D novels, the power of faith. The concept is not identical, but roughly so understood.

There are four kinds of divine-way method. First, the ancestral divine way, and the Three Imperishables divine way and the patriarchal divine way that grew from it. Second, enfeoffment methods, gathering the mind of the multitude to make gods and Buddhas — what we usually call the incense-fire divine way; including commoner enfeoffment, court enfeoffment, and founding a religion to cultivate godhood. Third, spreading religion to gain divine-way merit, and after death being enfeoffed as a divine officer. Fourth, leaning on gods, Buddhas, immortals, and ancestors, using one's own spirit-qi or cultivation to grow the deity-seed until one is made a god or Buddha avatar with some self-awareness. The second and third may overlap: what the dan school calls the immortal-god and earthly immortal — merit in the Dao, conduct among men, merit and conduct complete, summoned to ascend and become a heavenly immortal — is essentially enfeoffment as a divine officer.

The ancestral divine way is one source of the divine way; ancestral gods and nature gods are the earliest deities of this world.

The ancestral god is assembled from descendants' admiration and remembrance of the ancestor, and his power comes from the same. This too is incense-fire thought, of course, but a comparatively pure kind, very lightly polluted. So the ancestral god's advantage is low pollution and a clear head. Nor is there between ancestor and descendant the abuse of mutual enslavement of god and man that worship-faith brings. But his defects are no smaller: his dharma-power is weak; even with many sons and grandsons he rarely receives the incense of a standard incense-fire god. In a clan of thin numbers the ancestral god tends to perish. For with sparse descendants and diminished offerings, or across generations, earlier ancestors — left with only a name — lose their independence and merge into a common "ancestor" body. To mend this defect, people began slaughtering the three sacrificial beasts or slaves and feeding their souls to the ancestors to devour, sustaining and strengthening the ancestral god's dharma-power. But this method pollutes the ancestors rather heavily and costs much; the ordinary household cannot afford it.

When the wu religion declined, its orthodox heir Confucianism, having shed religion, held to benevolence and righteousness, and could all the less slaughter slaves wholesale to feed ancestors. Hence arose the Three Imperishables divine way: the three imperishables of life spoken of in the Zuo Zhuan — "Highest is setting up virtue; next, setting up merit; next, setting up words — these long endure, and are called imperishable."

Purely bloodline offerings were changed: by virtue, merit, and words, let descendants remember, admire, and long for one. The Three Imperishables divine way was originally a most perfect divine way — solving the ancestral way's problem of incense quantity without heavy pollution. But virtue and merit easily degenerate, in posterity's offerings, into the incense-fire divine way.

And among them only the truly outstanding can be imperishable, forever remembered by posterity.

Another variant of the ancestral divine way is the patriarchal divine way. Basically identical to the ancestral way — only descent of blood is replaced by descent of craft and descent of knowledge, greatly enlarging the number of juniors who make offerings. And the patriarchal way spun off another road: the ancestor-master seeds his juniors, absorbing their cultivation fruits to strengthen himself, even to surpass himself — or takes the juniors under his banner, expanding his power in the dharma-realm.

Of the second kind: at first the incense-fire divine way was commoner enfeoffment — based on things of nature the people worshipped, sun and moon, great trees and mountains; by their own natures, incense-fire thought-power conferred divine office, and they became nature gods among incense-fire gods, like the sun god, moon god, tree god, mountain god. Only afterward came worship of various beings and persons, gradually taking a personal-god shape. Even nature gods developed personal forms.

But in China divine authority must bow to royal authority. Commoner enfeoffment is not necessarily acknowledged by the court; acknowledged, the god is orthodox; unacknowledged, the worship is licentious and the god a wild deity. Even so grand a Daoist figure as the Jade Emperor □□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□ was only formally acknowledged by the court in the Song, once even replacing Haotian Shangdi to accept the court's sacrifices. But in the end he is reverenced only within one religion; the Heaven-and-Earth orthodox emperor remains Haotian Shangdi.

The legitimacy of the court's enfeoffment power lies in the legitimacy of its regime; the legitimacy of the regime lies in the people's recognition and the mandate of Heaven. The ancients said, the mandate of Heaven is the mind of the people; and also said, set your heart for Heaven and Earth.

So the court's enfeoffment power comes in the end from the people, and through the power of the regime guides the people's faith. The people's recognition of the court and imperial power is itself faith-force, incense-fire; this faith-force of incense grants the court the strength to suppress all gods and Buddhas. Hence the regime is called the sacred vessel of the altars. The regime's emblems — the nine tripods, the imperial seal — are likewise called sacred vessels of the altars. The edict of enfeoffment may be called a representative of the sacred vessel: the acknowledgment of immense dharma-power held by emperor and court can grant godhood to the one enfeoffed. It is precisely by holding the power of enfeoffment that the emperor is supreme in the three realms. Above emperor and imperial power stands only Haotian Shangdi and Houtu Huangdiqi, who stand for the cosmos and the Earth. Since ancient times ghosts and gods have had ranks; ghosts and gods must revere high officials and the emperor — the celestial court's ministers are ministers in the emperor's presence. So unless emperor or high official degrades himself to worshipping ghosts and gods, they cannot come near him; without a heavenly-emperor's godhood, whoever comes must kneel.

Even if successful, the great defect of the mutual enslavement of god and man cannot be escaped. The divine-way cultivator who becomes a god rather than a subordinate god should aim at independent awakening — but I do not believe they can ultimately escape the bonds of incense-fire.

The greatest test is keeping clear-headedness; otherwise the soul becomes jian — and even if it returns to the body, not scattered, one becomes a fool or a madman, and lives not long. Now information spreads fast; beyond founding a religion there are other options — singers, actors, celebrities can cultivate by this method and be enfeoffed after death. But if the fame cannot last, they fall from the altar and turn to ghost-jian, less even than a yin-spirit.

So of enfeoffments by various methods: some are pure mind-of-the-multitude; some mind-of-multitude plus the person's own soul; some mind-of-multitude plus a remnant soul; some merely mind-of-multitude plus gathered traces left in the world by the long-dispersed. Yet they themselves, if they have a bit of awareness, firmly hold that they are that former person. Perhaps some age-old god suddenly realizes he is not that person — I think it possible.

As for whether any soul can stay wholly independent, using incense-fire without being stained by it — no such thing exists.

Further: if a god accomplished through the divine way defies the mind of the multitude, he rebels against the ground of his own existence; incense-fire backlash follows, harming his self-awareness, even expelling him or splitting him off from the incense-fire — a wretched end. But defying the collective will is not easy; at minimum one needs great strength of will and great control of the people's incense-fire.

Third: those enfeoffed as divine officers or servant-gods through religious merit — authority and spirit-qi alike come from the deity's grant. Awareness and dharma-power both are given and taken at will. □□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□. But this is also the easiest way of long life — if that state can be counted long life.

Fourth: largely composed of self-power cultivation — there are even wholly self-power methods that can attain the way — but with a seed or a possessing thing on one's body, leaning on gods, Buddhas, ancestors, it can only count as divine-way cultivation.

Though half self-power, between the divine way and self-power there is no middle road: it is either one or the other. Incense-fire, whether one receives offerings or makes them, if one's cultivation is not of a certain level, is like poison — touch it and you enter the divine way. It is not only trancing shamans, burning incense, and kowtowing that count superstition; empowerment, seeding, even visualizing gods and Buddhas are divine-way cultivation. More than that: your pious faith will also gain you a deity-seed.

Burning incense, kneeling, chanting sutras, chanting names — not to train your perseverance, □□□□□□, but pretexts for incense-fire offerings. Fall into that pit and you walk the divine way; cultivate to the top and you are a god or Buddha's split-spirit, forever missing the true way.

It is not a question of whether the religions hold this or not — facts like these certainly cannot be spoken, and they may not understand them themselves. Once you believe, body and mind gradually receive the god-Buddha seed, or grow one of themselves, beyond your own will's control.

Whether the link forms by worship or by borrowed dharma-power, your essence-qi-spirit core, your cultivation core, becomes the god's, the Buddha's, the ancestor's; cultivation slowly grows this core, and what is yours shrinks; your thinking is influenced, actively or passively, and tends toward assimilation. At the end you still have some self-awareness, but the core of soul and spirit is no longer fully under your control. With ancestors, of course, the ancestor may be kind and never reclaim it — but fundamentally not-in-your-own-hands is the fact. Gods and Buddhas, when incense-fire is not lacking, leave you some freedom; when truly needed, they straightaway reclaim the split-spirit.

But cultivation by the spirit-qi of gods, Buddhas, or ancestors is much easier than pure self-power — so many willingly take seeding and empowerment. In most eyes, being not-in-one's-own-hands beats being scattered to ash and smoke. And with someone above pulling you up, the success rate is far higher than self-power. So I in fact understand those who enter the divine way: on the basis of understood principles, let each choose their own road. It is all for cultivation; different choices are all understandable — but not supported.

Daoism did not believe in gods at the start; now most believe — most treat it as a profession, earning incense money to get by; few truly study Lao and Zhuang and believe in cultivation. Seeding and empowerment are mostly done by gods, Buddhas, or ancestors through a master — the seed given directly, no evocation needed, much stronger than an evoked seed. Or obtained through worshipping gods, reading scriptures, or ritual.

Easiest of all is pious faith, which slowly evokes it. Somewhat devout adherents evoke a split-spirit of a god, Buddha, or dharma-protector into the body — that is the god-Buddha seed. Some, once influenced, shift from accepting self-power to deep Buddhist faith. Some people's obstinacy need not be deep attachment, □□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□, but subconscious influence. If you do not care, it does not hinder eating, drinking, or living; you cannot change the situation, so you may try accepting it. You acknowledge it exists — can't you also practice inviting the god to enter you?

In the dan path there are divine-way dan methods: with some attainment in life, after death one can become a subordinate god or officer of a high deity. Some so-called pure wuwei methods — their wuwei is nothing but sitting still; by self-power they cannot succeed. Their daily practice is in essence feeding that seed with their own spirit-qi; the seed is a higher-grade spirit. Some need not even cultivate by dan stages — enough nourishment and it is grown. Some toil at self-power dan stages, but in the end, stained by seed or faith, can only walk the road to subordinate god. Pitiable.

Cultivate the Buddha-dharma and you are influenced by it; hence the need to break it by scolding Buddha and cursing ancestors. Enfeoffment carries establishment — the police ghosts and gods with establishment can manage the thuggish ghosts and gods without, lest the thugs smash and loot. □□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□. Some divine-way cultivators know the pros and cons, even hold clear and complete lineages — but it is easier; highly attractive; one can eat, drink, and earn money. This is the choice made. Those who choose clearly are not fools: one dies either way; in life take the benefits, after death continue the soul's life; pay and return — no loss. Only, it is not cultivating the Dao. □□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□.

Among divine-way methods there is one that cultivates by eating ghosts — a note here in passing. When a ghost dies it becomes jian; eat too many ghosts and one's own soul gradually turns toward the nature of jian. The purer the cultivator's spirit, the broader the upward road. Tempering gang is already a last resort, much less tempering sha, much less incense-fire thought-power. Hence incense-fire is poison, severing the path. Hence the divine way, compared with self-power cultivation of Buddha and Dao, is lower — insiders call it falling into the divine way. But it is far easier than self-power, far more comfortable — and very self-assured. What is the nature of jian? A madman among men is already past reasoning; a madman among ghosts you may imagine. This influence hardly shows while one has a body, but is extremely hard to resolve; once the body is lost, it is over at once. With abundant incense-fire it is alleviated — but that is drinking poison to quench thirst. □□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□. The jian tends to scatter; sustained by abundant faith-force, it is kept existing. So religious people can still strengthen soul by eating ghosts □□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□; no matter. But this method, like founding-a-religion cultivation, has a great defect: spirit and form separate, the flesh corrupts — cultivate the faster, die the sooner.

In sum: the purely incense-fire sort — merit from preaching alone, brisk up five flights of stairs without effort — have the least independence, small attainments, and small office. The half self-power who believe in gods, Buddhas, and ancestors, pulled up by them — this half divine way — have more independence and higher attainments, but pay more effort. The wholly self-power, wholly independent, attain the highest — with the lowest success rate.

I tell you these things so that you may have some understanding of the divine way. After all, one who cultivates self-power without understanding the divine way — how does he cultivate self-power? Again and again he is dragged underwater. But so long as you do not believe, do not touch it, no fear. Also: do not try to persuade others, do not try to change others; drop the savior complex, respect others' fates. Do not talk to me of universal salvation — you cannot even save yourself; what do you save, eh?

Let us mind ourselves, get the principles clear, choose our road, and stand firm.
